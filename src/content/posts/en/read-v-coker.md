## Case Facts

The claimant was a paper-stainer who had entered into a business arrangement with the defendant following the distraint of his trade goods for unpaid rent. The defendant dismissed the claimant on 22 March 1853. On 24 March, the claimant returned to the defendant's premises and refused to leave when ordered to do so.

At that point, the defendant gathered his workmen, who proceeded to surround the claimant. The workmen tucked up their sleeves and aprons and threatened to break the claimant's neck if he did not leave the premises. Fearing that the men were about to strike him, the claimant departed.

The case proceeded to trial before Talfourd J and involved four counts. The first count alleged an assault on 24 March 1853, consisting of the surrounding conduct and the threat described above. The second count alleged an assault on the morning of 22 April, on which the jury was directed to find for the defendant. The third and fourth counts concerned false imprisonment: the third arising from 22 April, when the defendant caused the claimant to be taken before a magistrate on a charge of wilful and malicious trespass (the jury awarded £20 in damages); and the fourth arising from 27 April, when the claimant was brought before a magistrate on a charge of stealing in a shop (the jury awarded £5 in damages). In relation to the first count, Talfourd J directed the jury to consider whether the defendant had an intention to assault the claimant and whether the claimant was apprehensive of personal violence if he did not retire. The jury found for the claimant on the first count, awarding damages of one farthing. The jury also found that there had been a partnership between the claimant and the defendant in the machinery, which underpinned the claimant's assertion of a right to be on the premises and to deal with the goods therein.

A secondary procedural issue arose as to whether the defendant was entitled to a notice of action under the relevant statutory provisions, specifically the provisions then in force under what would now be described as the applicable notice requirements for acts done in execution of statutory authority.

## Held

The Court of Common Pleas held that the conduct of the defendant's workmen — surrounding the claimant, tucking up their sleeves and aprons, and threatening to break his neck if he did not leave — amounted to an assault in tort. The combination of the menacing physical posture and the conditional verbal threat, delivered by a group of men with an apparent present ability to carry out that threat, was sufficient to constitute an assault. The claimant's apprehension of imminent personal violence, which caused him to depart, was well-founded on the facts.

On the secondary procedural point, the court held that in order to be entitled to a notice of action for a thing done in pursuance or in the execution of an Act of Parliament, it is not necessary that the defendant was at the time cognisant of the existence of the statute conferring such protection, nor that they were acting strictly in the execution of it.

## Ratio Decidendi

The ratio of this case is that a conditional threat of violence — "I will break your neck if you do not go" — combined with physical conduct demonstrating a present ability to carry out that threat (in this case, the defendant's workmen surrounding the claimant in a menacing posture with sleeves and aprons tucked up) amounts to an assault in tort. There is no requirement of actual physical contact; what is required is an attempt or offer, by force or violence, to do a corporal hurt to another, coupled with a present ability to carry it into effect.

The court confirmed, drawing on [Tuberville v Savage (1669)](/tuberville-v-savage) and Stephens v Myers, that words alone cannot amount to an assault. In [Tuberville v Savage (1669)](/tuberville-v-savage), a man laid his hand on his sword and said "If it were not assize time, I would not take such language from you" — the words expressly negated any intent to assault, and accordingly no assault was established. The function of words in the law of assault is therefore to clarify or negate the meaning of an otherwise doubtful act. Where, however, words accompany physical gestures or actions that demonstrate a present intention and ability to use actual violence, an assault may be complete.

## Obiter Dicta

In the course of delivering the court's reasoning, a notable observation was made to the effect that if a man enters a room, lays his cane on a table, and says to another "If you don't go out, I will knock you on the head," that would not constitute an assault. A mere threat unaccompanied by any gesture or action towards carrying it into effect falls short of the legal requirements for assault. The distinction between such a scenario and the facts of the present case lies in the presence of the surrounding workmen who, by their physical conduct, demonstrated both the means and the apparent intention to carry out the threat immediately.

By analogy, it was observed that a sheriff's officer who enters a room and says to a man "You are my prisoner" thereby effects an arrest, even without any physical seizure — illustrating that the relationship between words and conduct is contextual, and that accompanying physical circumstances may be determinative of legal liability.
