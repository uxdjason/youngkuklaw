## Case Facts

In this case, St Helens Borough Council brought judicial review proceedings against Manchester Primary Care Trust (PCT) concerning the funding responsibility for the care of PE, an adult woman in her mid-thirties suffering from Dissociative Identity Disorder. The case has no connection whatsoever to child protection or parental rights; it is a public law dispute between two statutory bodies over which of them bore financial responsibility for PE's care under the relevant legislative framework.

PE had been known to St Helens Borough Council since her birth in 1975, and a care order had been made in 1978. The care arrangements in place at the time of the proceedings had been operational since April 2000. The annual cost of her care was approximately £675,000 — an exceptional figure that reflected the complexity and intensity of her needs. PE was represented throughout both the first-instance and appellate proceedings by the Official Solicitor, who expressed concern about the disturbing effect that protracted litigation was having upon PE herself.

The central dispute arose from a decision made by Manchester PCT's assessment panel on 30 November 2006. The panel met for three hours and twenty minutes, systematically considered the Strategic Health Authority's eligibility criteria for NHS continuing healthcare, and ultimately concluded that PE's needs were not primarily health care needs. On that basis, except in respect of physiotherapy and certain other specific health care matters, the PCT declined to fund her care. Responsibility therefore fell upon St Helens Borough Council under the social care framework governed by the [National Assistance Act 1948](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1948/29/contents), rather than upon the NHS under the [National Health Service Act 2006](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2006/41/contents).

St Helens Borough Council challenged that determination by way of judicial review, advancing five grounds: first, that the PCT had inaccurately recorded the views of the multi-disciplinary team; second, that it had failed to take account of all relevant material, particularly the reports of Dr Land; third, that it had misapplied the eligibility criteria; fourth, that it had misapplied the criteria relating to shared funding arrangements; and fifth, that the decision was irrational having regard to PE's unique, complex, and fluctuating condition. Importantly, there was no challenge at any stage to the lawfulness of the eligibility criteria themselves — the challenge was directed solely at the manner in which those criteria had been applied.

At first instance, High Court refused permission to bring the judicial review, rejecting all five grounds of challenge. St Helens Borough Council appealed to the Court of Appeal.

## Held

The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal and upheld the decision of Beatson J. The PCT's determination that PE's needs were not primarily health care needs was upheld as a lawful exercise of its statutory authority, and the judicial review challenge was refused in its entirety.

The court affirmed that the PCT's role in considering questions of health care eligibility is primary, having regard to the way in which the relevant legislation is framed. Beatson J had rightly rejected the submission that a court conducting judicial review should itself make a substantive determination as to the merits of competing expert conclusions reached by two public authorities, even where those conclusions were mutually inconsistent. The appropriate standard of review remained that of Wednesbury unreasonableness, derived from the principles articulated in [Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd v Wednesbury Corporation [1948]](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWCA/Civ/1947/1.html): a decision will only be impugned where it is so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have reached it.

The Court of Appeal further held that the PCT's reasoning withstood scrutiny on that standard. In particular, the panel had found that PE's care arrangements were unusual but not unique, and that the exceptional complexity of her situation resulted from the breakdown of her relationship with the local authority rather than from anything intrinsic to her clinical care needs. That reasoning was upheld as rational.

The panel's judgement was further strengthened by the introduction, from 1 October 2007, of a new National Framework for NHS Continuing Health Care, which now required local authorities and PCTs to agree a dispute resolution procedure that would not, as a first step, involve court proceedings. This development rendered the litigation in the instant case particularly striking as an example of what the new framework was designed to prevent.

## Ratio Decidendi

The ratio of this case establishes several important public law principles. First, the statutory authority of an NHS body such as a PCT to make determinations of continuing healthcare eligibility is primary, in the sense that the relevant legislative framework — principally the [National Health Service Act 2006](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2006/41/contents) — confers that responsibility upon the NHS body rather than upon the court or upon the local authority. Second, the appropriate judicial review standard for challenging such determinations is [Wednesbury](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWCA/Civ/1947/1.html) unreasonableness. Courts will not substitute their own assessment of expert evidence for that of the decision-making body, nor will they resolve disputes between two apparently rational but inconsistent expert conclusions reached by different public authorities. Third, where an NHS body has conducted a systematic and structured assessment of eligibility criteria, its conclusions are entitled to considerable deference absent demonstrable irrationality, procedural impropriety, or illegality.

These principles reflect the broader constitutional position that the courts' supervisory jurisdiction in judicial review is concerned not with the correctness of administrative decisions on their merits, but with their lawfulness. Where Parliament has entrusted a particular determination to a specialist statutory body, the court will not usurp that function by substituting its own view.

## Obiter Dicta

The Court of Appeal, in opening its judgement, delivered a pointed observation about the nature of the proceedings before it. The court noted that two publicly funded public authorities were engaged in expensive litigation to determine which of them should bear the cost of PE's care, and remarked that the money for the care and the money for the litigation were all coming out of the same public purse. This was a clear judicial admonition directed at inter-authority disputes of this character, signalling the court's discomfort with the deployment of public resources on adversarial litigation between statutory bodies that are collectively funded by the taxpayer.

This observation, whilst not strictly part of the ratio, carries significant weight as judicial guidance. The subsequent introduction of the National Framework for NHS Continuing Health Care from October 2007 — requiring mandatory agreement of a dispute resolution procedure as a precondition to litigation — demonstrates that the concern expressed by the courts in this case was shared at policy level. The case thus stands not only as authority on the [Wednesbury standard](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWCA/Civ/1947/1.html) in the continuing healthcare context, but also as a landmark prompt for the development of structured alternative dispute resolution between public authorities in this field.
