## Case Facts

This case was a consolidated appeal heard by the House of Lords involving two separate appellants: Robert Matthew Ireland and Anthony Christopher Burstow.

In the Ireland appeal, the appellant admitted making a large number of telephone calls to three women, remaining silent when they answered. A psychiatrist who examined the women confirmed that each had suffered psychological damage as a result of the repeated silent calls. Ireland pleaded guilty to three counts of assault occasioning actual bodily harm contrary to [s 47 Offences against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100/section/47) and was sentenced to a total of three years' imprisonment on 10 March 1995 at the Crown Court at Newport, Gwent. The Court of Appeal dismissed his appeal and certified the following point of general public importance under [s 33 Criminal Appeal Act 1968](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/19/section/33): "Whether the making of a series of silent telephone calls can amount in law to an assault."

In the Burstow appeal, the appellant conducted a sustained harassment campaign against a victim, which included making silent telephone calls, sending offensive cards and menacing notes, and making uninvited appearances at the victim's home and workplace. A consultant psychiatrist confirmed that the victim was suffering from a severe depressive illness as a result. Burstow pleaded guilty to unlawfully and maliciously inflicting grievous bodily harm contrary to [s 20 Offences against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100/section/20) and was sentenced to three years' imprisonment. The Court of Appeal dismissed his appeal and certified the following point of general public importance: "Whether an offence of inflicting grievous bodily harm under [s 20 Offences against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100/section/20) can be committed where no physical violence is applied directly or indirectly to the body of the victim."

Both appeals were heard together by the House of Lords.

## Held

The House of Lords dismissed both appeals.

In relation to Ireland, their Lordships held that a silent telephone call can constitute an assault where it causes the victim to apprehend immediate and unlawful violence. An assault may be committed by words or gestures alone, and the same principle extends to deliberate silence in circumstances where such silence induces the relevant apprehension in the victim's mind. The decisions of the Court of Appeal in both cases were affirmed.

In relation to Burstow, the House of Lords held that the word "inflict" in [s 20 Offences against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100/section/20) does not require the direct or indirect physical application of force to the victim's body. Psychiatric injury inflicted through a course of harassment is capable of constituting grievous bodily harm for the purposes of s 20.

Across both appeals, the House of Lords held that recognisable psychiatric illnesses fall within the phrase "bodily harm" as used in [ss 20 and 47 Offences against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100/contents), having regard to contemporary medical knowledge and the best current scientific understanding of the relationship between the body and psychiatric injury. [R v Clarence (1888)](https://www.commonlii.org/uk/cases/UKLawRpKQB/1888/175.html) was distinguished.

## Ratio Decidendi

The ratio of this decision rests on two principal propositions.

First, recognisable psychiatric illness amounts to "bodily harm" within the meaning of [ss 20 and 47 Offences against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100/contents). The House of Lords applied the reasoning in [Bourhill v Young [1943]](/bourhill-v-young), in which Lord Macmillan acknowledged the close relationship between body and mind. The court also drew on [R v Chan-Fook [1994]](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWCA/Crim/1993/1.html), in which the Court of Appeal had already confirmed that psychiatric injury could constitute actual bodily harm, provided it was a recognisable clinical condition rather than mere emotion, distress, or panic. The holding confirmed that the law should reflect contemporary scientific understanding; the human body and mind are not to be treated as separate and distinct entities for these purposes.

Second, an assault does not require the direct application of physical force, nor even spoken words — it may be committed through conduct or deliberate silence, provided the circumstances are such as to cause the victim to apprehend immediate and unlawful violence. In Ireland's case, the silent telephone calls placed the three women in immediate fear of such violence. The fact that Ireland could not physically reach the victims at that moment did not negate the immediacy of the apprehension; the calls were calculated to produce fear, and they did so. This analysis draws on the broader principle, confirmed in [Smith v Hughes [1871]](/smith-v-hughes) and endorsed in subsequent tort and criminal law contexts, that the law looks to the effect of the defendant's conduct on the victim's mind. For the purposes of [s 47 Offences against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100/section/47), where the assault occasions actual bodily harm in the form of recognisable psychiatric injury, the offence is made out.

On the s 20 question arising from Burstow, the House of Lords held that "inflict" does not carry a materially different meaning from "cause" in this context, and that the word does not import a requirement of direct or indirect physical violence. The court applied the reasoning of Lord Mackay of Clashfern LC in [R v Mandair [1995]](https://www.iclr.co.uk/document/1991004240/casereport_62457/html) and confirmed that a conviction under s 20 may stand where the grievous bodily harm consists of severe psychiatric illness inflicted through a course of harassment involving no physical contact.

It is important to note that [s 18 Offences against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100/section/18) was not in issue in either appeal, and the ratio is confined to ss 20 and 47.

## Obiter Dicta

The leading opinion was delivered by Lord Steyn, who made several observations beyond those strictly necessary for the disposal of both appeals. Notably, he addressed the relationship between the words "inflict" (used in [s 20 Offences against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100/section/20)) and "cause" (used in s 18), observing that there is no radical difference in meaning between the two terms for the purposes of s 20. Although this observation was not essential to the outcome, it carries significant weight as an authoritative statement on the construction of the 1861 Act and has informed subsequent academic and judicial treatment of the provision.

Lord Steyn also observed, in the context of the Ireland appeal, that the proposition that an assault can be committed by words alone — and, by extension, by silence — represents a development of the common law that is both principled and necessary to address modern forms of threatening and harmful conduct. The traditional requirement that an assault involve a gesture or overt act was not regarded as an insuperable barrier; the critical question is always whether the defendant's conduct caused the victim to apprehend immediate and unlawful violence.

These observations are significant for law students because they indicate the House of Lords' willingness to develop the law of non-fatal offences against the person in a manner responsive to evolving social circumstances and scientific understanding, without requiring legislative intervention.
