## Case Facts

In this case, the incident took place on or about 8 May 1886, at approximately nine in the evening. James Coultas, Mary Coultas, and John Wilson (Mary's brother) were travelling in a buggy from Melbourne to Hawthorn. Upon reaching a level crossing operated by the Victorian Railways Commissioners, the gate-keeper negligently opened the gates and invited the party to cross, notwithstanding that it was dangerous to do so. As the buggy was partly on the up line, a train was seen approaching at rapid speed. James Coultas drove on and managed to get the buggy clear; the train passed close to the back of the vehicle without making contact. Mary Coultas fainted and fell forward into her brother's arms.

Medical evidence established that Mary Coultas suffered severe nervous shock as a result of the fright, and that her subsequent illness was a consequence of that shock. One medical witness described her condition as a profound impression on the nervous system; another was unable to detect any physical injury and attributed her symptoms entirely to nervous shock.

The jury found that the defendants' servant had negligently opened the gate and invited the plaintiffs to cross when it was dangerous to do so, and that the plaintiffs could not have avoided what occurred by the exercise of ordinary care. The jury awarded £342 2s. to James Coultas and £400 to Mary Coultas. Three questions were then reserved for the Full Court of the Supreme Court of Victoria: first, whether the damages awarded were too remote; second, whether proof of physical impact was necessary to maintain the action; and third, whether the female plaintiff could recover for physical or mental injuries occasioned by fright. The Full Court answered all three questions in favour of the plaintiffs, entering judgment accordingly. The Victorian Railways Commissioners appealed to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council.

## Held

The Privy Council allowed the appeal and ordered that judgment be entered for the defendants, reversing the order of the Supreme Court of Victoria. The Board held that damages arising from mere sudden terror, unaccompanied by any actual physical injury but occasioning a nervous or mental shock, are too remote to be recoverable in negligence. The first reserved question — whether the damages were too remote — ought to have been answered in the affirmative by the Full Court below.

The Board further noted that no precedent had been cited of a similar action having been maintained, or even instituted, in the English courts, and it declined to establish such a precedent. The question of whether proof of physical impact was strictly necessary to maintain the action was expressly left open.

## Ratio Decidendi

The ratio of this case rests upon the applicable rule governing remoteness of damage in negligence. The Privy Council approved the principle stated by the Master of the Rolls in [The Notting Hill (1884)](https://www.commonlii.org/uk/cases/UKLawRpPro/1883/51.html), to the effect that recoverable damages in negligence must be "the natural and reasonable result of the defendants' act; such a consequence as in the ordinary course of things would flow from the act." This rule, the Board held, applied equally to the law of Victoria.

Applying that test, the Board concluded that injuries arising from mere sudden terror — unaccompanied by physical impact or injury — cannot, in the ordinary course of things, be regarded as a natural and reasonable consequence flowing from the negligence of a gate-keeper in opening a level crossing at an inopportune moment. Such damages are therefore too remote to found a cause of action in negligence. The first reserved question ought to have been answered in favour of the defendants. Counsel for the respondents had argued in reliance upon [Jones v Boyce [1816]](https://www.commonlii.org/uk/cases/EngR/1816/872.pdf) and [Sneesby v Lancashire and Yorkshire Railway Company [1875]](https://www4.commonlii.org/uk/cases/UKLawRpKQB/1875/72.html); the Privy Council distinguished the latter on the basis that it concerned a palpable physical injury caused by a person seeking to escape from the defendant's violence, which was a materially different situation.

## Obiter Dicta

The Board observed that to hold such claims recoverable "would be extending the liability for negligence much beyond what that liability has hitherto been held to be." It further warned that allowing damages for nervous shock caused by fright alone, without physical injury, would open "a wide field for imaginary claims." These observations reflect what would later be characterised as the "floodgates" concern — namely, that permitting liability in such circumstances would expose defendants to an indeterminate and unverifiable range of claims grounded in purely subjective mental experiences.

The Board also expressly noted, without deciding, the question of whether proof of physical impact was necessary to maintain an action for psychiatric injury, recording this point as one to be resolved in future litigation. This case was subsequently departed from as English law on psychiatric injury developed; notably, [Dulieu v White [1901]](/dulieu-v-white) declined to follow the restrictive approach adopted here, and the gradual recognition of claims by primary and secondary victims was further elaborated in cases such as [Page v Smith [1995]](/page-v-smith) and [Alcock v South Yorkshire Police [1991]](/alcock-v-south-yorkshire-police).
