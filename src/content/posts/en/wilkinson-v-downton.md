## Case Facts

On the evening of 9 April 1896, the defendant visited the home of the plaintiff, Mrs Wilkinson, whilst her husband Thomas Wilkinson had gone out to a race-meeting that day. As what was described as a practical joke, the defendant falsely told the plaintiff that her husband had been "smashed up in an accident" and was "lying at The Elms at Leytonstone with both legs broken." He further told her to go at once in a cab with two pillows to fetch him home.

The effect upon the plaintiff was severe. She suffered a violent shock to her nervous system, producing vomiting and other more serious and permanent physical consequences, at one time threatening her reason, and entailing weeks of suffering and incapacity, as well as expense to her husband for medical attendance. Importantly, the court expressly found that these physical and nervous consequences were not in any way the result of previous ill-health or weakness of constitution, and there was no evidence of any predisposition to nervous shock or any other idiosyncrasy — the plaintiff was in an ordinary state of health and mind at the time.

The plaintiff brought an action in the Queen's Bench Division. The matter came before Wright J as a further consideration following trial with a jury. The jury awarded two separate heads of damages: 1s. 10½d. for railway fares incurred by the plaintiff in sending persons to Leytonstone to investigate, and £100 for the injury caused by the nervous shock.

## Held

The court upheld both awards. The sum of 1s. 10½d. in respect of railway fares was considered clearly recoverable on the basis of misrepresentation — the defendant had made a false statement intended to be acted upon, to the damage of the plaintiff, bringing the claim squarely within the principle in [Pasley v Freeman (1789)](https://www.commonlii.org/uk/cases/EngR/1789/1703.pdf). However, Wright J was of the view that this basis alone might be insufficient to sustain the £100 award for nervous shock, and accordingly the court articulated a separate and broader ground for liability.

The court held that the defendant's conduct constituted an actionable wrong on the basis that he had wilfully done an act calculated to cause physical harm to the plaintiff and had in fact caused such harm. There being no justification alleged for the act, the claim was established. The full award of £100 damages for nervous shock, together with the sum for railway fares, was therefore affirmed.

## Ratio Decidendi

The ratio of this case is that a person who wilfully does an act calculated to cause physical harm to another — that is, to infringe that person's legal right to personal safety — and who thereby in fact causes physical harm, commits an actionable wrong. As Wright J stated: "The defendant has wilfully done an act calculated to cause physical harm to the plaintiff — that is to say, to infringe her legal right to personal safety, and has in fact thereby caused physical harm to her. That proposition without more appears to me to state a good cause of action, there being no justification alleged for the act."

A central feature of the ratio is the imputation of intention. Because it was difficult to imagine that such a statement, made suddenly and with apparent seriousness, could fail to produce grave effects upon any but an exceptionally indifferent person, Wright J held that an intention to produce the harmful effect must be imputed to the defendant. The defendant's act was accordingly treated as wilful in the legally relevant sense.

Critically, Wright J drew a distinction between legal malice and actual malicious intent, stating that "this wilful injuria is in law malicious, although no malicious purpose to cause the harm which was caused nor any motive of spite is imputed to the defendant." The absence of any subjective desire to harm the plaintiff was therefore no defence; the law regards as malicious any wilful act of this character.

On the question of remoteness, the court held that it was no answer in law to say that more harm was done than was anticipated, for that is commonly the case with all wrongs. The connection between the defendant's false statement and the physical harm suffered was sufficiently close and direct. The ratio is therefore grounded not in mere foreseeability of harm, but in the wilful nature of the act and its calculated tendency to infringe the plaintiff's personal safety, combined with actual causation of physical harm.

## Obiter Dicta

Wright J considered and distinguished several authorities in the course of his reasoning, and offered additional observations that go beyond the strict ratio.

First, he distinguished [Victorian Railway Commissioners v Coultas (1888)](/victorian-railway-commissioners-v-coultas) on two grounds: that case involved no element of wilful wrong, and the illness suffered there was not so direct and natural a consequence of the defendant's conduct as in the present case. He further noted that the authority of [Victorian Railway Commissioners v Coultas (1888)](/victorian-railway-commissioners-v-coultas) had been treated as open to question in [Pugh v London Brighton and South Coast Railway Co [1896]](https://www4.commonlii.org/uk/cases/UKLawRpKQB/1896/115.html). The significance of this distinction is that nervous shock — that is, mental shock not accompanied by any immediate physical impact — could found an action in the appropriate circumstances, provided the other elements were present.

Second, Wright J considered and distinguished [Allsop v Allsop (1860)](https://www.commonlii.org/uk/cases/EngR/1860/661.pdf), which had been approved by the House of Lords in [Lynch v Knight (1861)](https://www.commonlii.org/uk/cases/EngR/1861/822.pdf). In those cases, illness resulting from slander was held not to constitute sufficient special damage to support an action. Wright J distinguished them on the grounds that the reasons given — namely the absence of any precedent in slander cases and the policy concern against trivial actions — had no application to the present case, where the act was wilful and the physical harm substantial.

Third, Wright J briefly considered whether the £100 award might be sustained on the basis of fraud or deceit under the principles in [Pasley v Freeman (1789)](https://www.commonlii.org/uk/cases/EngR/1789/1703.pdf). He ultimately preferred the wilful act and physical harm ground as the more appropriate basis, though he acknowledged the fraud basis as a potential alternative for the railway fares element.

Finally, Wright J offered an illustrative obiter example: if a person in a precarious state of health is falsely told that their physician has said they have but a day to live, and death or serious aggravation of illness results, such a case might found a criminal prosecution or a civil damages claim. This demonstrates that the principle articulated in the ratio is not confined to practical jokes but extends to any wilful false communication calculated to cause physical harm.
