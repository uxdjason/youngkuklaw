## Case Facts

The appellant, Doreen Violet Hill, brought this action as administratrix of the estate of her deceased daughter, Jacqueline Hill, aged 20, under the [Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1934](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo5/24-25/41/contents). Jacqueline Hill had been attacked one night in a city street in West Yorkshire and died from her injuries. Her attacker was Peter Sutcliffe, the serial killer known as the "Yorkshire Ripper", who was subsequently convicted of her murder. The Chief Constable of West Yorkshire was sued under [s 48(1) Police Act 1964](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1964/48/section/48/enacted) on the basis of vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of his officers in failing to apprehend Sutcliffe before he could carry out further attacks.

The claimant alleged that West Yorkshire Police had been negligent in the conduct of their investigation into the series of murders and attacks attributed to Sutcliffe, and that, had they exercised reasonable care, they would have identified and apprehended him before Jacqueline Hill became his final victim.

On 19 December 1985, Sir Neil Lawson, sitting as a judge of the Queen's Bench Division, struck out the writ and statement of claim as disclosing no cause of action. The Court of Appeal dismissed the plaintiff's appeal, affirming the strike-out. The House of Lords then heard the appeal on 8, 9, and 10 February 1988, with judgment delivered on 28 April 1988.

## Held

The House of Lords dismissed the appeal and affirmed the decision of the Court of Appeal. The court held that West Yorkshire Police owed no duty of care to Jacqueline Hill in respect of their investigation and suppression of crime. The claim was dismissed on two grounds: lack of proximity and public policy.

On the proximity question, the majority held that although it was reasonably foreseeable that Sutcliffe, if not apprehended, would be likely to harm young female members of the public, foreseeability alone was insufficient to establish a duty of care. The fact that Jacqueline Hill had been young and female did not of itself place her in a special or exceptional position distinguishable from the public at large. There were no additional characteristics capable of establishing the requisite proximity between the police and her specifically.

On public policy, the court held that the police are immune from actions in negligence in respect of their activities in the investigation and suppression of crime. This constituted a further, independent ground for dismissing the appeal.

## Ratio Decidendi

The central ratio of the case is that the police do not owe a general duty of care to individual members of the public in the course of investigating and suppressing criminal activity. This was established on two cumulative grounds.

**First ground — lack of proximity:** Lord Keith, with whom Lord Brandon, Lord Oliver, and Lord Goff agreed, applied the dicta of Lord Diplock in [Home Office v Dorset Yacht [1970]](/home-office-v-dorset-yacht) in analysing the proximity question. Drawing also on the reasoning in [Yuen Kun Yeu v A-G of Hong Kong [1988]](https://www.bailii.org/uk/cases/UKPC/1987/1987_16.html), Lord Keith held that although reasonable foreseeability of harm is a necessary condition for imposing a duty of care, it is not sufficient. There must also be a sufficiently proximate relationship between the parties. The police could, in principle, owe a duty of care to an individual where their failure to apprehend a criminal had created an exceptional added risk — one different in incidence from the general risk to the public at large — so as to establish the requisite proximity. However, on the facts, no such exceptional added risk was made out. Being young and female was a characteristic shared by a very large segment of the public, and it was not a sufficiently special attribute to establish proximity between the police and Jacqueline Hill in particular.

**Second ground — public policy:** As a further and alternative basis for dismissing the appeal, the House of Lords held that the police enjoy immunity from negligence claims in relation to the investigation and suppression of crime. Several public policy considerations supported this conclusion. Imposing a duty of care would expose the police to a wide and indeterminate class of potential claimants — all members of the public who might be harmed by criminals. Moreover, the threat of litigation would have a detrimental effect on police decision-making, causing officers to act defensively and to allocate resources to protecting themselves from suit rather than to effective law enforcement. The principle of immunity analogous to that recognised in relation to barristers in [Rondel v Worsley [1969]](https://www.bailii.org/uk/cases/UKHL/1967/5.html) was applied in support of this ground.

The [two-stage test from Caparo v Dickman [1990]](/caparo-v-dickman) — foreseeability, proximity, and fairness — and the earlier approach in [Donoghue v Stevenson [1932]](/donoghue-v-stevenson) both informed the court's analysis. The decision established that mere foreseeability that police inaction might allow a criminal to harm an unidentified member of the public is insufficient, without more, to generate a duty of care running to any particular individual.

## Obiter Dicta

Lord Templeman, concurring in the result, placed particular emphasis on the public policy ground. He stated that the efficiency of a police force cannot be investigated by an action for damages but only by an inquiry instituted by the national or local authorities which are responsible to the electorate for that efficiency. This observation, whilst forming part of his concurring reasoning on public policy, encapsulates a broader constitutional point: that democratic accountability, rather than tort litigation, is the appropriate mechanism for scrutinising police performance.

The decision in this case has been subject to significant subsequent consideration. In [Osman v Ferguson [1993]](/osman-v-ferguson), the Court of Appeal held itself bound by this authority and declined to impose a duty of care even where the police had received specific warnings about a particular individual. The European Court of Human Rights subsequently examined the compatibility of the blanket immunity with the right to a fair trial under Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights in [Osman v United Kingdom (Case No 87/1997/871/1083)](/osman-v-uk), ultimately finding that the automatic application of such an immunity was disproportionate. In [Van Colle v Chief Constable of Hertfordshire Police [2008]](/van-colle-v-hertfordshire-police), the House of Lords revisited the scope of police liability, and the interaction between this case and [Smith v Sussex Police [2008]](/smith-v-sussex-police) further refined the circumstances in which a duty of care may arise in the context of police failure to protect individuals from third-party harm.
