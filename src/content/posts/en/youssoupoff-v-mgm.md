## Case Facts

Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer Pictures Limited produced and distributed a film depicting events surrounding Rasputin and his murder by a group of aristocratic conspirators. The film combined fictional and real-life elements, featuring among its characters a fictional Russian princess named Princess Natasha. Her storyline bore a close resemblance to the life of Princess Irina Alexandrovna Youssoupoff, the wife of Prince Felix Youssoupoff, who was one of Rasputin's real-life killers. The central defamatory imputation was that Princess Natasha — whom the plaintiff and her witnesses identified as representing Princess Irina — had been seduced or ravished by Rasputin.

The plaintiff, Princess Irina Alexandrovna Youssoupoff, brought an action in defamation against the defendant film company. The jury found in her favour and awarded damages of £25,000. MGM appealed on three grounds: first, that there was insufficient evidence that reasonable viewers would identify Princess Natasha as Princess Irina; secondly, that the portrayal was not capable of being defamatory; and thirdly, that the damages award of £25,000 was excessive.

## Held

The Court of Appeal dismissed MGM's appeal on all three grounds and affirmed both the jury's verdict and the £25,000 damages award.

On the question of identification, the court held that there was sufficient evidence from which the jury could properly conclude that reasonable viewers would identify Princess Natasha with Princess Irina. Where such evidence exists, identification is a question of fact for the jury to determine.

On the question of whether the portrayal was capable of being defamatory, the court held that the imputation that Princess Irina had been seduced or ravished by Rasputin was capable of being defamatory. The court confirmed that a statement need not impute moral fault or culpability to the claimant in order to constitute defamation; it is sufficient that the statement tends to cause the plaintiff to be shunned or avoided by right-thinking members of society.

On the question of whether a cinematographic film constitutes libel or slander, the court held that a film — as a recorded and repeatable medium combining photographic images and spoken words — amounts to permanent matter and therefore constitutes libel rather than slander. This was a significant holding that confirmed the application of libel principles to the medium of film.

On the question of damages, the court declined to interfere with the jury's award, affirming that appellate intervention on damages in libel cases is limited to circumstances involving legal error or an award so extreme as to indicate improper direction or perversity.

## Ratio Decidendi

The ratio decidendi of this case establishes two related but distinct principles concerning the scope of defamation.

First, defamation is not confined to statements that expose a person to hatred, ridicule, or contempt by reason of some moral discredit on their part. A statement is also defamatory if it tends to cause the plaintiff to be shunned or avoided by right-thinking members of society, even where the plaintiff bears no moral fault whatsoever. This materially broadens the definition of defamation beyond the traditional formulations articulated in cases such as [Parmiter v Coupland [1840]](/parmiter-v-coupland) and [Sim v Stretch [1936]](/sim-v-stretch).

Secondly, the question of whether the plaintiff was seduced or ravished — that is, whether she bore any degree of moral responsibility for the imputed conduct — is entirely irrelevant to whether the statement is defamatory. As stated at page 587 of the report, the court observed that it is immaterial whether the libel suggests that the plaintiff was seduced or ravished, since the question of whether she is more or less morally culpable does not bear upon the question of whether she has been defamed. What matters is the effect of the statement upon her social standing and reputation.

In applying these principles, the court took judicial notice of the fact that a woman of whom it has been said that she has been ravished, albeit against her will, suffers in social reputation and in the esteem in which she is held by the world. This application of the principle confirmed that the imputation was capable of causing Princess Irina to be shunned or avoided by right-thinking members of society, notwithstanding the absence of any moral fault on her part.

The case also established the important ancillary principle that a cinematographic film constitutes libel rather than slander, by reason of its permanence as a recorded medium.
