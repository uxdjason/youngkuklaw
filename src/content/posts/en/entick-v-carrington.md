## Case Facts

In this case, the plaintiff, John Entick, a clergyman and political writer, brought an action in trespass against Nathan Carrington and three other King's messengers following an unlawful search of his home and seizure of his papers.

On 6 November 1762, the Earl of Halifax, one of His Majesty's principal Secretaries of State and a member of the Privy Council, issued a general warrant directed to the defendants. The warrant authorised them to search for Entick, who was described therein as the author or a person concerned in writing several seditious papers entitled *The Monitor, or British Freeholder* (issues No. 357, 358, 360, 373, 376, 378, and 380), published for J. Wilson and J. Fell in Paternoster Row, and said to contain gross and scandalous reflections and invectives upon His Majesty's Government and upon both Houses of Parliament.

On 11 November 1762, the defendants entered Entick's dwelling-house in the parish of St Dunstan Stepney without his consent. They remained on the premises for approximately four hours, during which time they broke open doors, boxes, chests, and drawers, read and examined his private papers, and took away a large quantity of books and papers. Entick alleged damage to the sum of £2,000. He was discharged from custody on 17 November 1762, but the civil action for damages proceeded in respect of the unlawful entry, search, and seizure.

The defendants pleaded justification, contending that they had acted under the lawful authority of the Secretary of State's warrant. A special verdict was returned by the jury, leaving the relevant legal questions to be determined by the court. The case was heard in Westminster Hall and the judgement was delivered on 2 November 1765 in the Michaelmas Term of 6 George III.

## Held

The court held that the general warrant issued by the Earl of Halifax was unlawful and without legal authority. The Secretary of State possessed no power at common law or by statute to issue a warrant authorising a general search of a person's home and seizure of their papers. Accordingly, the defendants' entry into Entick's dwelling-house and their seizure of his books and papers constituted an unlawful trespass for which no legal justification could be advanced.

The court rejected the argument that executive necessity or long-standing administrative practice could supply the legal authority that was otherwise absent. The defendants' conduct was found to be indefensible in law, and the plaintiff was entitled to succeed in his action.

## Ratio Decidendi

The ratio decidendi of this case is the foundational constitutional principle that all executive and state power must be grounded in positive legal authority, whether derived from the common law or from statute. Where no such authority exists, any interference by agents of the Crown with a person's property or liberty is unlawful.

The court articulated this principle in terms that have since become central to the rule of law in English constitutional law. If the executive claims a power to act, that power must be found in the law; if it cannot be found there, it does not exist. This principle is captured in the famous formulation from the judgement: 'If it is law, it will be found in our books. If it is not to be found there, it is not law.'

The state cannot justify an interference with private rights by asserting necessity, executive convenience, or prior practice. The mere fact that Secretaries of State had previously issued such warrants, and that those warrants had been acted upon without challenge, could not in itself render the practice lawful. Legality must rest on a recognised legal foundation, not on administrative habit or assumption of power.

This principle remains one of the cornerstones of English public law. It underpins the doctrine that government bodies may act only within the limits of their lawful authority, and it continues to inform contemporary approaches to judicial review as seen in [R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017]](/unison-v-lord-chancellor), the rule of law as discussed in [R (Jackson) v A-G [2005]](/r-jackson-v-a-g), and the protection of individual liberty against arbitrary state action.

The case is also significant for establishing that the privacy of a person's home and papers is protected against executive intrusion in the absence of clear legal warrant.
