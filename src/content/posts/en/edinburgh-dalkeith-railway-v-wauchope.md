## Case Facts

In this case, the appellant was the Proprietors of the Edinburgh and Dalkeith Railway Company, and the respondent was John Wauchope of Edmonstone. The dispute arose from the provisions of two private Acts of Parliament governing the railway's operations and the tolls payable to Wauchope as a landowner whose estate the railway traversed.

The first Act, [Edinburgh and Dalkeith Railway Act 1826](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukla/Geo4/7/98/contents/enacted), established the railway. By s 20 of that Act, Wauchope was granted a way-leave of one halfpenny per ton upon all goods and articles upon which a tonnage duty was chargeable under the Act, passing through his lands, with the exception of coals, minerals, corn, and the produce of his own estate. s 85 of the same Act further empowered the company to levy tonnage duties, including in respect of every carriage conveying passengers, or goods or parcels not exceeding five hundredweight, a sum not exceeding sixpence per ton per mile.

Wauchope had originally opposed [the 1826 Bill](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukla/Geo4/7/98/contents/enacted) in Parliament, but withdrew his opposition after clauses protecting his interests — including the halfpenny tonnage way-leave contained in s 20 — were inserted for his benefit. For several years thereafter, he accepted payments from the company in respect of goods and parcels only. In 1835, however, he claimed a way-leave on the tonnage of carriages when loaded with passengers, asserting that his statutory entitlement extended to passenger traffic as well.

A second Act, [Edinburgh and Dalkeith Railway (Dalkeith and Leith Branches) Act 1834](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukla/Will4/4-5/71/contents/enacted), was subsequently obtained by the company. s 29 of that Act repealed the rates and duties on carriages conveying passengers that had been granted under the recited Acts, and s 30 substituted a new toll not exceeding threepence per mile per person conveyed. Wauchope contended that this second Act did not apply to him, or alternatively that it could not be made applicable to him because he had not been given proper notice of its introduction in Parliament.

The Lord Ordinary issued an interlocutor in favour of Wauchope on 16 June 1837, finding that the two Acts were not inconsistent and that his right to a way-leave on passenger tonnage subsisted. In his accompanying note, the Lord Ordinary expressed doubt as to whether due parliamentary notice had been given to Wauchope before the passage of the second Act, and indicated sympathy for the proposition that rights previously established could not be removed by a private Act without due notice. He declined, however, to decide the case on that basis. The Court of Session affirmed the Lord Ordinary's interlocutor on 14 December 1837. A further interlocutor of 2 March 1839, finding that tonnage was properly calculated on carriages rather than on passengers separately, was subsequently altered by the Inner House by decree of 4 July 1839. The railway company appealed to the House of Lords.

## Held

The House of Lords affirmed the decision of the Court of Session and held in favour of Wauchope on all material points. First, the company was empowered under [the 1826 Act](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukla/Geo4/7/98/contents/enacted) to levy a tonnage duty on carriages according to their weight when containing passengers, and Wauchope's entitlement under s 20 was to be calculated by reference to the tonnage so levied. Second, Wauchope's prior acceptance of payments on goods and parcels only did not estop him from subsequently claiming his full statutory entitlement in respect of passenger tonnage; his acquiescence in lesser payments did not amount to a construction of the Act fatal to his claim. Third, on the parliamentary sovereignty point, the House of Lords held that a party interested in the subject-matter of a private Act of Parliament is bound by its provisions even where that Act was introduced and passed without due notice being given to him, and that courts have no power to hold a duly passed Act of Parliament invalid on account of any failure of notice during its parliamentary passage.

## Ratio Decidendi

The primary ratio of the case concerns the statutory construction of the tonnage and passenger duty clauses in [the 1826 Act](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukla/Geo4/7/98/contents/enacted). The House of Lords determined that, on the true construction of ss 20 & 85 of the [Edinburgh and Dalkeith Railway Act 1826](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukla/Geo4/7/98/contents/enacted), the company was liable to levy a tonnage duty on carriages conveying passengers and that Wauchope's way-leave entitlement extended to that tonnage. The prior acceptance by Wauchope of lesser payments did not alter his statutory rights.

The constitutionally significant ratio addresses parliamentary sovereignty. The House of Lords affirmed the absolute binding force of a duly enacted Act of Parliament, whether public or private. Once a Bill has been passed by both Houses and has received Royal Assent, it becomes law and binds all parties affected by it. Courts of law have no jurisdiction to examine the internal proceedings of Parliament or to inquire into whether a private Act was passed in accordance with proper parliamentary procedure, including whether adequate notice was given to interested parties. This principle operates irrespective of whether the affected party had actual knowledge of the Bill's progress. The ruling thus confirmed and reinforced what is now regarded as a foundational principle of parliamentary sovereignty in the constitutional law of the United Kingdom, and was later affirmed in [Pickin v British Railways Board [1974]](/pickin-v-british-railways-board), in which the House of Lords restated that courts will not look behind the face of an Act of Parliament to examine the manner in which it was passed.
